01 Introduction

What is ajrakh?

“A double-sided, block-printed textile worn as caste dress by cattle herders in the desert regions of Kachchh and Thar … and Sindh … made by Khatri artisans.” Eiluned Edwards, Textile History 47(2), 2016
TechniqueResist and mordant printing with carved wooden blocks, then dyeing in indigo and madder
BipuriTraditionally printed on both sides so the pattern lines up exactly. Single-sided work is ekpuri
MakersThe Khatri community of dyers and printers, for about 400 years in Kutch
Time14–16 stages over 2–3 weeks in Kutch; up to 21 stages and about a month in Sindh

Where the name comes from

AzrakArabic for blue or indigo. The derivation most scholars and craft bodies give.
“Aaj rakh”“Keep it today.” A local story among printers, because each layer has to rest before the next.
A-jharatSanskrit, “that which does not fade”, as recorded by Gaatha.

02 Origin & history

A pattern older than the name

2000–1900BCE · MOHENJO-DARO, SINDH

The steatite “Priest-King” from Mohenjo-daro wears a cloak carved with trefoils and circles that still show traces of red. It is now in the National Museum of Pakistan (NMP 50-852).

“Block printers will identify the trefoil motif as kakkar, a cloud pattern common to the traditional Ajrakh patterns made in Sindh and Kachchh.”
Khamir, “Khatris & Community”

A cotton fragment from Mohenjo-daro, carbon-dated to 1760 BCE ±115, is reported to show madder dyeing (Ashmolean, Barnes 1997). Linking the statue to ajrakh is an interpretation, not a proven fact.

Sources: Wikipedia “Priest-King” (NMP 50-852) · Khamir exhibitions · Ashmolean Museum, R. Barnes 1997
02 Origin & history

From the Indus to Ajrakhpur

1010 CE ±55
Trade to EgyptIndian, probably Gujarati, resist- and mordant-dyed cottons reached Egypt. The earliest at Oxford's Ashmolean is dated to c. 1010 CE ±55; most are 10th–15th c. Relatives of ajrakh, not labelled as such.
1585–1631
Sindh to KutchRao Bharmalji I invited Khatri printers from Sindh. Family tradition names Jinda Jiva as the first to settle at Dhamadka on the Saran river (dates vary).
1855–83
Into museumsThe V&A, London collects Sindh ajrakh, including a piece from Thatta (IS.216-1883). Today 17 of its records match “ajrakh”.
1947
PartitionThe border cut the Kutch–Sindh trade and the herders' routes. Khatris from Mirpur, Sindh settled in Barmer, Sanganer and Bagru.
1950s–70s
Loss and revivalCheap chemical dyes halted natural dyeing for about 15 years. It resumed as 1970s government schemes brought artisans and designers together.
2001
EarthquakeThe Bhuj earthquake devastated Dhamadka and raised iron in the water. Led by Dr Ismail Khatri, the community bought land near Bhuj together and founded Ajrakhpur.
2024
GI certificate“Kutch Ajrakh Print of Gujarat” is registered as a Geographical Indication (filed 2023, certificate 22 Apr 2024).
Sources: Ashmolean (Barnes 1997) · Sahapedia (M. Khan) · Warwick oral history, Dr Ismail Khatri · V&A · Textiles Committee, GoI · Khamir · IP India GI 1050

04 Raw materials

Earth, plants, iron and water

Cloth

  • Latha: plain cotton, once hand-spun and handwoven. Kora latha in Sindh
  • Today also mulmul, modal, Gajji silk, mashru, Chanderi, Kala cotton
  • Cotton takes the colours best

Mordants

  • Harda (myrobalan, Terminalia chebula): tannin, the first mordant
  • Fatakdi (alum): fixes the red
  • Lokhand + gud (scrap iron + jaggery): fermented into kat, the black

Dyes

  • Indigo: natural, or synthetic today
  • Majith (madder) or alizarin, boiled with dhaori flowers
  • Haldar (turmeric), dadam (pomegranate), ruharb (rhubarb) for yellow, green, brown

Resists

  • Chuna + baval no gund (lime + gum arabic): keeps whites white
  • Gach: clay, millet flour, alum and gum, a mud resist
  • Sawdust or dung dusted on to stop smudging

Scouring & pastes

  • Traditionally gissi (camel dung), soda ash, erandi (castor oil); today often plain water
  • Kachuka no lot: tamarind-seed powder that thickens the black and alum pastes

Water

  • The Saran river's water suited dyeing and washing
  • Iron in water dulls colours. That is why the craft moved after 2001
Sources: Karolia & Buch, IJTK 7(1) 2008 (local names) · D'Source, IIT Bombay · Gaatha · Khamir · Anokhi Museum via Wandering Silk · Sahapedia (Bilgrami)
04 Tools

The block is the pen

Blocks are hand-carved, often in Pethapur near Gandhinagar (some printers now carve their own), from teak (sagwan), sheesham or rohida; sources differ on the wood. A simple block takes 2 days; a fine one 12–15. Brass pins make the tiny dots.

05 Motifs & patterns

How the cloth is built

Kutch layout per the Anokhi Museum: “The ends of the cloth are dyed red, whilst the longer sides are kept blue.” Varadarajan counted 15 basic Kachchh designs, and there are said to be over 200 traditional combinations.

Mizan: balance and order. “The design square is perfectly divided into quarters and then further divided into sixteen parts.”

No figures. Following Islamic principles, no human or animal forms. Symmetry often radiates from a central star.

Architecture on cloth. The shapes echo “jali windows and trefoil arches” (Khamir).

Bipuri. Printed identically on both sides; legend says so herders dressing before dawn always wore the pattern outward.

“There is no centre, there is no periphery, and it keeps on expanding without any end.”Dr Lotika Varadarajan, Sahapedia, 2014

Sources: Karolia & Buch, IJTK 2008 · Gaatha · Anokhi Museum via Wandering Silk · Khamir · Sahapedia (Varadarajan) · JDSSI 2025. Diagram drawn for this deck

06 Technique & process

Two weeks before the first print

PreparePrintDye & reveal
  1. 01

    Saaj Scouring

    The cloth is soaked in camel dung, soda ash and castor oil, kept overnight, half-dried in the sun and soaked again. This repeats 7–8 times “until the cloth foams when rubbed”, then it is washed.

  2. 02

    Kasanu Harda bath

    Dyed in a cold solution of myrobalan (harda), which turns it yellow and acts as the first mordant. It is dried on both sides and the excess powder is beaten off.

“Two weeks can be spent preparing the cloth before printing even begins.” Maiwa. No two Khatri families follow exactly the same sequence, and even the names of pastes differ between lineages.

Sources: D'Source, IIT Bombay (Saaj, Kasano) · Travels in Textiles, process as told by Dr Ismail Khatri · Karolia & Buch, IJTK 2008 · Maiwa · Anokhi Museum via Wandering Silk
06 Technique & process

Printing what the dye must not touch

PreparePrintDye & reveal
  1. 03

    Khariyanu Rekh outline

    A resist of lime and gum arabic is printed on both sides to outline everything that will stay white.

  2. 04

    Kat Iron black

    Scrap iron and jaggery ferment for 1–3 weeks (sources vary), then are thickened with tamarind seed. Printed paste turns black as it meets the harda's tannin.

  3. 05

    Kan / pa Alum mordant

    Alum paste is printed where red must appear. A fugitive colour is added so the printer can see where to place the block.

  4. 06

    Gach Mud resist

    Clay, millet flour, alum and gum are printed over the design, dusted with sawdust or dung, and left to dry for 3–4 days.

Sources: D'Source, IIT Bombay (Khariyanu, Kat, Gach) · Karolia & Buch, IJTK 2008 (Phulli/Kan) · Anokhi Museum via Wandering Silk (Pa) · Project LooM
06 Technique & process

Blue, then red, then light

PreparePrintDye & reveal
  1. 07

    Bodaw Indigo

    Dipped in the sunk vat, the cloth comes out green and “rapidly becomes blue as oxygen meets the dye particles in the fibres”.

  2. 08

    Vichharnu Washing

    Washed to clear loose dye, in the river once, in stepped tanks today. Sources differ on how much resist comes off here.

  3. 09

    Rang Red boil

    Boiled 2–4 hours with madder or alizarin and dhaori flowers. Alum areas turn red, iron turns black, and the whites appear.

  4. 10

    Minakari “Double work”

    Gach, indigo and red are repeated for deeper, brighter colour.

  5. 11

    Tapano Sun bleaching

    Soaked in camel dung and laid in the sun for about 3 days to clear the whites. Recorded by the Anokhi Museum; called tapai in Sindh.

Sources: D'Source · Travels in Textiles (Bodaw) · Karolia & Buch, IJTK 2008 · Anokhi Museum via Wandering Silk (Tapano) · Sahapedia (Bilgrami) · V&A IS.7-2007, Dr Ismail Khatri's stage sampler
07 Cultural importance

Worn from the cradle

For pastoral communities ajrakh was never decoration. It was caste dress, a turban (pagdi), a lungi and a shoulder cloth, and a sign of who you are.

Newborns

Used “as a hammock for infants” in Sindh, and as linings in cradles for kids among the Maldharis.

Weddings

“An Ajrakh turban was a gift for the Maldhari groom at the time of marriage.” Given for Eid and to bridegrooms.

Every age

Named cloths by age, from paniyu for boys under six to bepoti for elders. Khatri women's prints changed at marriage and widowhood.

Every season

More indigo in summer because it is cooling, more red in winter for warmth. Herders also used it as a bundle for the road.

Traditionally worn by Muslim Maldharis; Khamir and the Kachchh district site also name Rabaris and Ahirs.

Sources: Khamir exhibitions · District Kachchh, Govt of Gujarat · Arab News 2013 · Gaatha · Homegrown (citing “Made in Kachchh”) · Edwards 2016

11 Four things that matter now

Protected, practised, prized

GI tag1050GI application no.

“Kutch Ajrakh Print of Gujarat”

  • Filed 31 Jan 2023; journal 19 Dec 2023; certificate 22 Apr 2024
  • Applicant: Ajarakhpur Hastkala Vikas Sangathan · Classes 24 & 25
  • 95 authorised users. Only Ajrakhpur and Dhamadka printers may call it “Kutch Ajrakh”
Livelihood~100Khatri families in Ajrakhpur & Dhamadka

A family trade, ten generations deep

  • Skills passed father to son for 9–11 generations; women now print, finish and lead labels
  • “Even the lowest-earning artisan makes up to Rs 1,000 a day” (Sufiyan Khatri, c. 2016)
  • Threats: water scarcity, iron in wells, chemical dyes, screen-print copies
Global17V&A, London records matching “ajrakh”

From medieval Egypt to Santa Fe

  • Museum holdings: V&A, Ashmolean, Textile Museum (Washington)
  • ROM's touring Global Threads at the Bowers Museum, Dec 2025–Apr 2026
  • Khatri workshops in Canada, the UK, the US and Japan; post-GI orders from Uzbekistan, USA, France
Market2–10×hand-block over imitation: about 2–5× for Indian fabric, 5–10× at one Sindh retailer

What the real thing costs

  • Hand-block cotton fabric ₹300–1,250/m (silk up to ₹3,290); sarees ₹3,000–22,000
  • A 2.5 m dupatta sells for ₹3,000–7,000 and costs ₹2,000–2,500 to make
  • Imitation “ajrakh print” sells online for ₹150–300/m

How to spot the real thing
  • Printed on both sides, near-identical front and back
  • Slight irregularities from hand placement
  • Natural-dye depth; stars in bright white
  • “If you find a fabric being sold at unrealistically low rates, it is most certainly a fake.” iTokri

12 Conclusion

A cloth that keeps the sky, and a people

Ajrakh has survived migration, Partition, synthetic dyes and an earthquake because it is more than a print. It is a way of knowing water, plants and time, held by a few Khatri families and worn as identity from Sindh to Kutch.

Keep the methodNatural dyes and hand-block resist printing make it ajrakh, not the look. Double-sided work is the traditional ideal.
Credit the makerGI protection works only if buyers and designers name the artisans.
Mind the waterIts future depends on clean water as much as on demand.
